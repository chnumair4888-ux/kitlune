How EMI is calculated: formula and worked example

Written by the Kitlune team•Last updated 23 October 2026

EMI usually means equated monthly instalment: a regular monthly payment used to repay a loan over a set term. For a standard fixed-rate amortizing loan, each payment contains interest plus repayment of principal. Early payments generally contain more interest because the outstanding balance is larger; as the balance falls, more of the same payment goes toward principal.

This explanation is educational. Real lenders can include fees, insurance, taxes, rounding rules, changing rates or other terms that make their quoted payment different from a simple calculator.

The three inputs that drive the calculation

Principal (P) is the amount borrowed. Monthly interest rate (r) is the annual nominal rate divided by 12 and then converted from a percentage to a decimal for a basic monthly model. Number of payments (n) is the loan term in months.

For a standard fixed-rate amortizing loan, the monthly payment can be represented as:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

If the interest rate is zero, the formula above would involve division by zero, so the simple result is principal divided by the number of payments.

Worked example

Assume a loan principal of 100,000, an annual interest rate of 12%, and a one-year term with 12 monthly payments. The monthly rate is 12% ÷ 12 = 1%, or 0.01 as a decimal. Here P = 100,000, r = 0.01 and n = 12.

First calculate (1 + r)n: 1.0112, which is approximately 1.126825. The numerator is 100,000 × 0.01 × 1.126825, approximately 1,126.825. The denominator is 1.126825 − 1, approximately 0.126825. Dividing gives a monthly payment of about 8,884.88.

Across 12 payments, that is about 106,618.56 in total payments. Subtracting the original 100,000 principal gives about 6,618.56 of interest in this simplified example. Small differences can appear because of rounding.

Why 12% annual interest does not mean paying 12% of the original loan every year

In an amortizing loan, interest is normally calculated on the outstanding balance for each period. After a payment reduces the principal, the next period begins with a smaller balance. That is why simply taking 12% of 100,000 and dividing it by 12 does not reproduce the standard EMI schedule.

Common mistakes

A frequent mistake is entering 12 as the monthly rate when 12% is the annual rate. Another is mixing years and months. Also avoid comparing loans only by monthly payment: a longer term can reduce the monthly payment while increasing the total amount of interest paid. Finally, an advertised rate may not represent every borrowing cost, so read the lender’s disclosure and fee information.

Frequently asked questions

Does a lower EMI always mean a cheaper loan?

No. A longer repayment period can lower the monthly payment while increasing total interest.

What happens when the interest rate is zero?

With no interest or fees, divide the principal by the number of monthly payments.

Why is my lender’s payment different?

The lender may use different timing, rounding or rate conventions, or include fees, insurance or other charges not modeled by a basic calculator.

Is the Kitlune result financial advice?

No. It is an educational estimate for understanding payment mechanics and comparing assumptions.
