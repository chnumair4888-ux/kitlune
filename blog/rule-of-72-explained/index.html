The Rule of 72 explained, and where it is not accurate

Written by the Kitlune team•Last updated 23 October 2026

The Rule of 72 is a mental-math shortcut for estimating how long it takes an amount to double when it grows at a steady percentage rate with compounding. Divide 72 by the annual percentage rate, and the result is an approximate number of years to double.

Estimated doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ annual percentage rate

It is useful because you can do it quickly without a calculator. It is not an exact law, and it should not be confused with a guarantee that an investment will earn a particular return.

Worked example at 8%

Suppose an amount grows at a steady 8% per year. The Rule of 72 gives 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. So the shortcut says the amount would roughly double in nine years.

Using the compound-growth equation, the exact doubling time for an 8% effective annual growth rate is a little over nine years. The shortcut is therefore close enough for a quick estimate, but an exact calculation is better when precision matters.

Another example at 6%

At 6%, the shortcut gives 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years. This is easy to remember and useful for comparing rates mentally. At 9%, it gives 8 years. At 12%, it gives 6 years.

These examples assume the rate stays constant. Real savings rates can change, and investment returns can vary from year to year or become negative. A changing return does not follow the simple shortcut neatly.

Why 72 works as an approximation

Exact compound doubling time comes from logarithms. The Rule of 72 replaces that calculation with convenient division. The number 72 is useful because it has many small divisors, so common rates such as 6, 8, 9 and 12 are easy to calculate mentally.

The approximation is especially handy for learning the relationship between rate and time: higher steady rates imply shorter doubling times, while lower rates imply longer ones. But that relationship does not remove risk or uncertainty.

Where the shortcut becomes less accurate

The Rule of 72 is an approximation, so its error changes with the rate and compounding convention. At rates far from the range where the shortcut happens to fit well, the difference from the exact calculation can become more noticeable. The shortcut is most accurate near 8%: at 8%, 72 ÷ 8 gives 9.0 years while the exact formula, ln(2) ÷ ln(1.08), also rounds to 9.0 years. It drifts at very low and very high rates because 72 ÷ rate is a convenient reciprocal approximation, while the exact relationship is logarithmic. The farther the rate moves from the range where those two curves closely match, the more noticeable the difference can become.

It can also mislead when the stated rate is nominal but compounding occurs several times per year. In that case, the effective annual growth can differ from the stated nominal rate. Make sure you understand which rate you are using.

It does not handle regular deposits

The doubling shortcut is easiest to interpret for one starting amount. If you add money every month, the balance can double partly because of your new contributions and partly because of growth. Saying “my money doubled” then does not tell you how much came from compounding.

For a savings plan with regular deposits, model the contributions separately. That gives a clearer view of your own deposits versus estimated growth.

It does not include fees, tax or inflation automatically

If an account or investment has fees, the net growth available to compound may be lower than the headline return. Taxes can also affect outcomes depending on the product and jurisdiction. Inflation is different again: an amount may double numerically while its purchasing power does not double.

The Rule of 72 does not make any of these adjustments by itself. You must choose a rate that represents the quantity you actually want to estimate.

Do not use it as a return prediction

If an investment earned 8% in one period, dividing 72 by 8 does not mean it will definitely double in nine years. Market returns are not fixed interest rates. A projection based on a constant rate is a scenario, not a forecast.

For uncertain returns, it can be more informative to compare several assumptions. Open the Compound Interest Calculator and try a lower, middle and higher rate while keeping the starting amount and time constant. The range shows how sensitive the outcome is to the assumption.

Worked comparison with exact compound growth

Imagine 10,000 growing at a fixed 8% annually with no deposits or withdrawals. After nine years, the compound formula gives 10,000 × 1.089, which is about 19,990. That is very close to double 10,000, which explains why the nine-year Rule of 72 estimate feels useful here.

But the important phrase is “fixed 8% annually.” Change the rate from year to year, add fees, withdraw money or make new deposits, and the simple doubling interpretation changes.

Common mistakes

Do not enter 0.08 into the shortcut when you mean 8%; divide 72 by the percentage number 8. Do not treat the result as exact. Do not use a historical investment return as if it were guaranteed. Do not forget that a nominal rate and effective annual rate can differ. And do not use doubling time alone to judge whether a financial product is suitable.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Rule of 72 exact?

No. It is a convenient approximation. Use compound mathematics for a more precise answer.

Can I use it for debt?

It can illustrate how a balance might grow under a steady compounding rate if nothing is paid, but real debts have payment schedules, fees and contract terms that require a fuller calculation.

Does it work with monthly contributions?

It does not separate new contributions from growth, so a savings calculator is more informative for regular deposits.

Does a nine-year estimate at 8% guarantee doubling?

No. It only describes a constant-rate mathematical scenario.
