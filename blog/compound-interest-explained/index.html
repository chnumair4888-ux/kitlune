Compound interest explained with numbers

Written by the Kitlune team•Last updated 23 October 2026

Compound interest means growth is applied not only to the original amount but also to earlier growth that remains in the account or investment. That makes time important: when growth stays invested, later periods can build on a larger balance.

Compounding is a mathematical mechanism, not a guarantee of profit. Bank rates can change, investments can lose value, and fees, taxes and inflation can affect what a future balance is worth.

A simple yearly example

Suppose 10,000 earns a fixed 10% per year and all interest stays in the account. After year one, 10% of 10,000 is 1,000, so the balance becomes 11,000. In year two, 10% is applied to 11,000, producing 1,100. The balance becomes 12,100. In year three, 10% of 12,100 is 1,210, giving 13,310.

If this were simple interest calculated only on the original 10,000, each year would add the same 1,000. The difference appears because compound growth includes earlier interest in the next calculation.

The basic formula

Future value = principal × (1 + periodic rate)number of periods

For annual compounding at 10% for three years, the calculation is 10,000 × 1.103 = 13,310. When compounding happens more frequently, the periodic rate and number of periods must match that frequency.

What regular contributions change

If you add money every month, there is no single starting date for all the money. The first deposit has more time to grow than the last deposit. A calculator handles that by applying growth period by period and adding contributions according to an assumed schedule.

For example, imagine starting with 20,000 and adding 2,000 at the end of each month. Even with a 0% return, after 12 deposits you would have contributed another 24,000, giving 44,000. With a positive return, the modeled balance can be higher because the starting amount and earlier deposits have time to earn growth. The exact result depends on the rate, compounding convention and deposit timing.

Rate, time and contribution: three different levers

A higher assumed rate increases projected growth, but it can also make a projection less realistic if the rate is chosen only to reach a desired number. More time gives existing money more periods to compound. Larger or more frequent contributions increase the amount of your own money working toward the goal. For planning, contribution and time are often easier to control than market returns.

Common mistakes

Do not confuse a percentage with a decimal: 8% is 0.08 in a formula. Make sure annual and monthly units are converted consistently. Do not assume a historical or advertised return will continue unchanged. Avoid ignoring fees and taxes when they apply. And remember inflation: a larger future number may not buy the same amount of goods and services that the number buys today.

Frequently asked questions

Is compound interest always good?

Compounding can help savings or investments grow, but it can also increase debt when unpaid interest is added to a balance.

Does monthly compounding always beat yearly compounding?

With the same stated nominal rate and otherwise identical assumptions, frequency can change the effective result. Real products may quote rates using different conventions, so compare their actual terms.

Are investment returns the same as interest?

No. Investment returns can fluctuate and may be negative. A fixed calculator rate is only a simplified assumption.

Does the calculator include inflation, fees or tax?

The basic Kitlune projection does not automatically model all of those costs, so interpret the result accordingly.
